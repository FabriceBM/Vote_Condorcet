Voting for a consensus with veto rights

A group must choose one option. Anyone may propose as many options as they wish (H1), everyone judges every option (H2), and everyone can veto (H3). The procedure must reach the best compromise (R1) and never get stuck (R2). The two requirements pull against each other: a veto protects minorities and blocks decisions.

The answer: QVC(θ), Condorcet with a blocking quota, θ = 1/3
  1. Each voter ranks every option and marks the unacceptable ones.
  2. Options vetoed by at least a share θ of the voters are excluded.
  3. If that excludes every option, the least-vetoed ones are kept.
  4. The Schulze winner among the options left is elected.

Why unanimity deadlocks: the status quo inside the hull

Each voter prefers options closer to their ideal point. A proposal y beats the status quo s for a voter on y's side of the perpendicular bisector of s and y. If s lies inside the convex hull of the ideal points, every such half-plane misses at least one voter: someone always prefers s, and under unanimity s stays (Proposition 2). With more voters, the hull covers s almost surely. In the simulations, unanimity keeps the status quo in 43% of elections with 3 voters, 86% with 11 and 99.6% with 101.

Why flooding matters: proportional veto against the blocking quota

Under an open agenda, a faction can add variants of its favourite. The proportional veto core gives a coalition a share of vetoes equal to its share of voters, counted in options, so every variant the majority adds dilutes the minority's vetoes (Proposition 3). Under the blocking quota a variant inherits the vetoes of its original, so it is excluded with it (Proposition 7). Examples E1 and E2: the majority adds A₂ and A₃, which it ranks just below A and the minority ranks last and vetoes.

In the simulations, three clones of a losing option change the winner of the proportional veto core in up to 54% of elections, of fallback bargaining in up to 36%, of Borda in up to 24%, and of Schulze and QVC in 0%.

The θ dial: majority at one end, minority protection at the other

A low quota gives every small group a veto: the fallback decides most elections, the result is close to approval voting, and small coalitions can steer it. A high quota never binds and leaves the majority alone. Between 25% and 40%, the veto excludes what a substantial minority rejects and the majority decides among the rest. One third sits there, close to the European Union's 35% blocking minority.

Simulation of the note: 101 voters, 8 options, 1,000 elections per electorate (300 for manipulability). Efficiency: 0 for the average option, 100 for the best; worst-off: the voter at the 10th percentile. A single veto is plotted at 1%.

What remains open

A clone-proof proportional veto?

The proportional veto core resists strategic blocking but not flooding; the blocking quota resists flooding but not strategic blocking. Can one rule have clone independence, veto power proportional to size and a non-empty core?

Fast procedures with hard vetoes

One- and two-round proposal procedures reach the Condorcet winner quickly with single-peaked preferences. With binding vetoes and several dimensions, nobody knows yet.

Do named vetoes work?

Responsibility changes votes in committees, and tacit consent keeps formal vetoes in check. A laboratory test of QVC with and without named, reasoned vetoes would settle it.

Real ballots

The simulations use stylised electorates. Ranked ballots with an acceptability line exist, in Debian's general resolutions among others, and would test the conclusions on observed preferences.
